How the ball trajectory is computed

A single phone camera cannot see depth. Everything below is about what you can still measure once you accept that — which turns out to be more than you would guess and less than a product would like. It also covers the ruler this pipeline deleted, which is the most instructive thing in it.

01The one thing one camera cannot see

A pinhole camera turns a 3D point into a pixel by dividing by depth. The projection the pipeline actually runs is this, with f_px the focal length in pixels and Zc the depth along the optical axis:

u = cx + f_px * (q · right) / Zc
v = cy − f_px * (q · up) / Zc

pipeline/ball.py:1077–1084

Depth appears only in a denominator, multiplied against the focal. A ball twice as far away and twice as large lands on exactly the same pixels. One camera has no way to tell those two worlds apart.

The consequence is the sentence this whole page orbits: depth transfers to speed one for one. If the depth is 25 % wrong, the speed is 25 % wrong. There is no averaging that helps, no number of frames that fixes it. The pipeline states it flatly:

“…measured Hough-circle diameter at 24–28 % relative depth error, and depth transfers to speed 1:1. Their disagreement is the reason the band exists; it is not a reason to trust either.”

docs/BALL.md:248–251

And, on a clip with no reference object at all:

“A metre is a metre: a single-basin clip with no goal in frame pins the arc's SHAPE and nothing about its SCALE.”

pipeline/ball.py:93–96

02The identity that saves half the answer

Here is the good news, and it is better than it first looks. The scale ambiguity does not contaminate the whole measurement. It contaminates exactly one axis.

Start with the ball's own diameter as a depth cue. A sphere of real diameter D that covers d pixels sits at depth

This is why the viewer's fixed panel can sit still while the scene stretches. On the Stanford clip that means these numbers are simply measured, at any focal you care to name:

Attribution, precisely

The cancellation identity is stated in the scene file this analysis produced — out/goal_anchored/scene_measured.json, generated by scripts/goal_ab.py — not in pipeline/ball.py. The pipeline never implements X = (x − cx)·D/d directly: it back-projects through a solved camera instead (ball.py:1087–1093) and treats the apparent radius as a soft weighted residual, r_model = f·R/Z (ball.py:1297–1299), rather than inverting it. The structurally identical algebra is in the pipeline for the goal — depth = f·goal_w / width_px at ball.py:1741. Everything on this page is either in the source or in that scene file; where the two differ, the difference is called out.

Same pixels, eight lenses

Because only depth scales, you can enumerate the entire answer as a one-parameter family. Goal distance comes out exactly linear in the focal; launch speed does not, because the two focal-free velocity components refuse to grow with it.

Why the goal cannot break the tie

A rectangle of known size in view ought to solve the camera. It does not here, because the goal is fronto-parallel: its post lengths differ by only 1.93 % and its crossbar is tilted 1.44°. With no perspective foreshortening to exploit, a rectangle projects as a rectangle at any focal/depth pair, and the PnP solve is degenerate. The pipeline detects this explicitly — post-length asymmetry under 0.04 and crossbar angle under 2.5° trips goal_fronto_parallel and it falls back to scale-only depth (ball.py:1735–1741). The goal pins the depth once you choose a focal. It cannot choose one.

03The two rulers that survive

Scale can only come from something visible whose real size is already known. As of 2026-08-21 the pipeline computes exactly two candidates, and the code is the authority (ball.py:1586–1590, the per_anchor dict):

Ruler 1 — a goal of known size

The only true reference, and the only thing that flips quality.metric_reference to true (ball.py:1926) or unlocks a point speed at all. Detection is a white mask (V > 160, S < 60), a probabilistic Hough line transform, verticals filtered to 70–110° and horizontals to under 20°, then solvePnP with SOLVEPNP_IPPE (ball.py:1707–1754). It enters the fit as a single heavily-weighted residual, weight 6.0 (ball.py:1454–1455).

Ruler 2 — the ball's own 22 cm

A FIFA size-5 ball is 0.22 m across (size 4 is 0.205 m). Its apparent radius gives depth through r = f·R/Z. This is an estimator, not a reference, and it is fragile: motion blur inflates the apparent diameter, which under-estimates depth, which biases the speed low. Radii are filtered with a Theil–Sen fit of 1/r against frame index, demoting anything more than 30 % off the line (ball.py:1321–1339). One measurement is exempt and carries the highest weight in the entire fit: the pre-contact ball sitting still on the ground, the one blur-free radius in the clip (ball.py:836–837).

Where the prose still disagrees with the code

The gravity removal (next section) was not fully swept through the documentation. BALL.md:233–246 still lists “Gravity as ruler” as metric anchor #2, and ball.py:36–39, :1906 and :1931–1932 still name it in docstrings and warning strings. The computed per_anchor dict has only diameter_kmh and goal_kmh. Trust the computation.

04The ruler that was deleted

This is the most useful thing in the pipeline, and it is a deletion.

Gravity is an attractive ruler. You know g = 9.81 m/s² for free, everywhere on Earth, without measuring anything. A ballistic arc's curvature against its timing therefore looks like a free metric scale: solve for the effective gravity, compare to 9.81, and the ratio hands you the scale factor.

It works if the arc's curvature comes from gravity alone. On a real struck ball it does not. Drag through the transonic-for-a-football regime and Magnus from spin both bend the same arc, and a single-view fit cannot separate either of them from a scale error. So the solver quietly folds the scale error into g and reports a number.

“The GRAVITY RULER IS GONE (2026-08-21). Using a known g to set metric scale cannot be separated from drag and Magnus on a real strike: this module's own effective-g came back at 14.69 m/s² on the reference clip, 1.5× real gravity, which is a scale error wearing gravity's clothes rather than a measurement. Scale now comes only from things actually visible in the frame — a goal of known size, or the ball's own 22 cm diameter.

Gravity remains in the DYNAMICS (see integrate_flight): the ball really does fall at 9.81 m/s² and removing that would stop the model being a trajectory at all. What is deleted is gravity as a RULER, not as a force.”

pipeline/ball.py:1576–1585 — the in-code tombstone, commit 36d6aa0

Two details make this more than a tidy-up.

The failure had been predicted, in the same repository, before it was acted on. BALL.md:193–196 already said the anchor “is not measuring gravity; it measures g + drag, and drag was mis-specified by up to a factor of two.” It took the solver returning 1.5 × g for the argument to land.

A cross-check built on it was worse than useless. A ratio of the two rulers' speeds was supposed to detect retimed footage — feed it an 8× slow-motion bake and it should return 8. Measured on a real bake it returned 1.02. The reason is that both “independent” rulers were re-solves against a camera geometry pinned to the primary fit, and the primary fit had already used the radius observations, so the gravity anchor inherited its scale from the diameter ruler through the pinned camera. Two rulers that were never independent cannot disagree usefully. It was deleted with the ruler; retimed clips are still caught by the duplicate-frame ratio, which flagged that same bake at 0.88 against a 0.50 threshold.

What replaced it

Nothing — deliberately. A residual sanity alarm survives: if the solved g_eff lands more than 50 % away from 9.81 the fit emits effective_g_..._beyond_what_magnus_alone_can_explain_metric_scale_error_suspected (ball.py:1627–1630). It is a smoke detector for misspecification, not a ruler. Note that the 14.69 which killed the ruler sits at 1.50×, exactly on that threshold's edge.

05What a ball-diameter ruler actually costs: 24–28 %

With gravity gone and no goal in frame, scale rests on the ball's apparent size alone. It is worth being precise about how good that is, because the answer is published and it is not good.

Van Zandycke & De Vleeschouwer (CVPRW 2022, Table 2) measured Hough-circle ball diameter against ground truth and found 24–28 % relative depth error. Combine that with the 1:1 transfer from section 1 and the consequence is immediate.

A number that looks similar and means something else

ball.py quotes 24–35 % in several warning strings. That is not the published Hough error — it is the observed disagreement between the two rulers on down-the-axis shots, which is the reason a band is emitted instead of a value. Two different quantities that happen to start with the same digits.

06Three trackers, one track

Before any geometry there has to be a track, and a small white ball against a bright sky over grass defeats any single detector. Three independent ones run on every frame and vote.

The interesting parts are the guards, each of which encodes a specific way the naive version fails:

  • A lone flow lock can never count as a detection. Its confidence is clamped to 0.28, just under the 0.30 detection threshold, because “a lone LK/NCC lock only bridges — it can self-confirm any drifting patch forever” (ball.py:956–969). It is allowed to keep the track alive across a few frames and nothing more.
  • A “moving” lock that has not moved is a distractor. If the predicted speed exceeds 3 px/frame but the candidate barely displaced, it is dropped (ball.py:947–955).
  • The ball must be seen to leave. Candidates for the ball at rest are tracked until departure; one that never departs is discarded. This is what rejects a screen-recording play button and spare balls on the grass (ball.py:683–732, :1858–1877).
  • Consistent motion is not enough to launch on. The launch search scores three-frame candidate triples by velocity consistency, then re-scores by Hough confirmation, because “a kicker's trailing white sock coasts consistently too, but never confirms as a circle” (ball.py:744–746).
  • Stabilisation does not transfer to the near field. The global background affine drifted a stabilised probe about 90 px off the ball on the prototype clip when the camera translated (ball.py:689–694), so the rest-ball tracker works in raw coordinates.

Below eight real detections the pipeline raises rather than fits; below twenty it refuses a point value and emits a band only (ball.py:1884–1889). The Stanford clip in the viewer has 21.

07Drag, and the soccer crisis

A football does not obey a constant drag coefficient over the speeds a person can kick it. It crosses the drag crisis — the laminar-to-turbulent transition of the boundary layer — right in the middle of the range, and its drag coefficient falls by a factor of three as it speeds up.

Getting this wrong is not a rounding error. At 100 km/h, a fixed Cd of 0.45 and the crisis value of 0.15 differ by 18.8 versus 6.3 m/s² of deceleration — 1.9 g against 0.6 g. Any fit that assumes a constant Cd across a strike has to absorb that mismatch somewhere, and where it absorbs it is the scale.

BALL.md:212–218. Note the deceleration is nearly flat from 54 to 81 km/h: the falling Cd almost exactly cancels the rising v².

Spin is modelled as a direction, never a rate

Magnus enters as at most two free accelerations, bounded to about ±1.4 g, and the default model carries only the lateral one. That choice is empirical, not aesthetic: on a roughly 6,000-segment real-soccer benchmark (ball3d, CVPRW 2026) a simple drag model beat drag-plus-Magnus on all five monocular settings, because “angular-momentum parameters remain poorly constrained by 2D reprojection residuals from a single viewpoint” (ball.py:45–54). Curl is read out as the sign of the quadratic term in the horizontal reprojection residual, reported only when its t-statistic clears 1.5 (ball.py:1391–1433). No field anywhere in the module carries a spin rate.

08Camera height and tilt: many answers, one pixel apart

Even with the scale settled, the geometry has a second problem. For a shot travelling roughly away from the camera, camera tilt trades against launch elevation, and camera height trades against depth, at almost no cost in reprojection error. The optimiser does not have one minimum. It has several, separated by less than the noise on the track.

Because free multi-start optimisation was found to miss the truth entirely — on a synthetic clip all nine free starts converged on one camera at 3.2 m / 24° while the true camera at 1.6 m / 12° sat 0.07 px away in RMS and was never even offered (ball.py:1477–1483) — the pipeline stopped searching and started enumerating. It solves a fixed 5 × 5 lattice of camera heights (1.0 to 3.5 m) and tilts (0° to 32°) against two speed seeds, 50 pinned solves, then refines the distinct survivors inside their own boxes and reports up to four (ball.py:1475–1510).

The honest part: the ranking is not yet defensible

On the synthetic clip with known ground truth, the four surviving basins span 1.4–3.2 m of camera height and 16.8°–29.5° of launch elevation with RMS differing by 0.1 px. The truth is inside the set — but it is rank 4 of 4, so the rank-1 values the file emits are 13.2° away from it. The documentation says so plainly: “ranking by drag-only solver cost is not yet a defensible ranking” (BALL.md:392–412). The corresponding test was widened from “truth is rank 1 within 4°” to “truth is somewhere in the set”, which is the weaker claim that is actually true.

One more measured surprise, from the goal-anchored A/B run on the Stanford clip: the true camera was 0.08 m off the ground, tilted 4.6° upward — outside the solver's default box on both axes, which searches heights from 0.8 m and tilts downward only. With the box widened, the two rulers on that clip disagreed by 63 %. A solver railed against its own bounds reports a solution pinned by a bound rather than by the data, and the pipeline emits exactly that warning when it happens (ball.py:1210–1219).

09Time: the container lies

Speed scales one-for-one with the assumed frame rate, exactly as it does with depth. And a video container will happily state its frame rate three different ways that do not agree — after a re-encode, an AirDrop, or a slow-motion bake, at least one of them is wrong.

The timebase assessment reads three independent statements about the rate — avg_frame_rate, r_frame_rate, and the median inter-packet presentation-timestamp interval — plus a duplicate-frame count, and reacts on a fixed table (ball.py:346–433):

signalthresholdreaction
avg_frame_rate vs r_frame_rate> 2 %point value refused; uncertainty ≥ 0.10
packet timestamps vs decoded rate> 2 %warn; uncertainty ≥ 0.15 — the caller's decoder may itself be at fault
timestamp interval spread (variable rate / speed ramp)> 5 %point value refused; uncertainty ≥ 0.25
duplicate-frame ratio≥ 0.50point value refused — a 2× retime lands on exactly 0.500

The Stanford clip trips the first row. The pipeline's own worked example of a worse case is an AirDropped clip claiming avg_frame_rate 30 against r_frame_rate 239.02 — an eightfold disagreement in a file that plays back perfectly.

10What the system refuses to claim

This list is enforced in code through a claims block, not left to the reader's judgement. The documentation's instruction to anyone wiring these numbers into a product is “gate on the claims block, never on the presence of a field” (BALL.md:9–12).

Refused outright

  • Any point value for launch speed with no metric reference object in frame. launch.speed_kmh is null and claims.point_speed is false. Ship the band.
  • Apex height, launch elevation and solved camera geometry as measurements, whenever the basins are ambiguous or there is no reference — because the competing basins differ by under 2 px RMS on tracks carrying 10–25 px of centroid error.
  • Any spin magnitude, ever. claims.spin_magnitude is hard-wired false. The comparison offered is that Hawk-Eye — calibrated and multi-camera — reports a spin bias of −100 ± 542 RPM.
  • Its own earlier accuracy tiers (±5–8 % / ±10–15 % / ±20–30 %), which are described as unvalidated engineering estimates. Of roughly 40 surveyed repositories and papers, not one reports a launch-speed error from monocular video.
  • “±5–10 % is typical for consumer video apps.” The two independent validations found go the other way: a cricket system at 2.6–3.1 % CV plus a +2.5–4 % systematic bias and judged not interchangeable with radar, and a tennis system at +6.8 % bias. Both had a known metric corridor this does not.
  • Anything below ±3 %, ever, from one phone — the criterion radar's own coefficient of variation on soccer kicks is 4.0–5.4 %.

Claimed, and defensible

The band. The refusal rule itself. The trajectory's shape. Curl and spin direction. The quality flags. And, most usefully for a product: session-relative ranking. A tennis system reaches an intraclass correlation of 0.970 while carrying a 6.8 % bias — which is the whole point. “Your hardest strike today” is defensible long before “your strike was 38.7 km/h” is.

Plus, on this particular clip, everything in the focal-free column: 5.37 m/s of lateral speed, 0.66 m/s of vertical speed, 2.37 m of lateral travel and 0.39 m of rise are measurements, not estimates — see the viewer.

The honest bottom line

There is no radar ground-truth set yet, so no accuracy figure anywhere in this system has been validated against an independent instrument. The documentation's own follow-up item is to buy a radar and build one, with the note: “Everything above is unfalsifiable without it” (BALL.md:504–506).
